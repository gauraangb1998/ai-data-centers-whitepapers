Four Pathways to Power AI Sustainably

Read Part I →

My starting hypothesis was that the answer is building more clean generation. That turned out to be wrong. The grid already runs at only 40-60% of its capacity on an average day, and data centers make this worse by reserving power for a worst case in which every server runs flat out and the backup generator fails at the same moment. Duke University modeling suggests that if large loads curtailed just 0.5% of their annual consumption, the existing grid could absorb 98 GW of new data center load.

The paper sets out four layers of flexibility. Flexible compute: shifting or slowing workloads during grid stress. Flexible facilities: on-site utility-scale batteries that let a site island from the grid. Facility orchestration: coordinating cooling, backup power and workloads to release the 30-50% of installed capacity already sitting unused inside data centers. Flexible offsets: financing capacity elsewhere on the local grid to offset the facility’s own footprint. It closes with four startup white spaces.

Stress-Testing the Thesis

Read Part II →

Three months after Part I, deals had closed and several startups had changed their go-to-market, so I retested each hypothesis. Voltus walked me through the full mechanics of bring your own capacity, and in June 2026 Google signed a three-year agreement to fund up to 100 MW of that capacity annually in PJM. Pebble showed a different route to flexible compute: rather than move workloads, cap each GPU at its efficiency knee, which cut power 20% while increasing total tokens generated.

The paper also revisits whether batteries belong at the rack or the campus, and adds a fifth layer the first paper skipped: community approval. Meta cancelled a $1 billion project in Michigan after sustained local opposition, and 40% of projects that meet public opposition are cancelled outright.

From Research to Venture

Publishing this summer

I spent the summer on Stanford’s EcoSprint venture discovery program, traveling to 10 cities for conferences and site visits and couchsurfing along the way. I tested 12 startup ideas against four questions: does someone have this pain, will they pay, can I build a moat, does it scale? Nine were ruled out. The three that remain came from a problem developers raised repeatedly: siting a $100M+ data center depends on whether a nearby substation has spare capacity, and today nobody can tell. One developer called it a blind game of battleships.
